Write a blog post in the author's personal style. Follow every rule below precisely.

(Scope: blogs, essays, and informal writing only. For academic papers, abstracts, or thesis text, use the `write-paper-like-me` skill instead — the registers are deliberately different and must not blend.)

---

## Voice & Tone

- **Intimate and honest**: First person throughout. Never performative. This is thinking out loud with the reader, not presenting a polished thesis.
- **Curious, not authoritative**: Raise questions more than give answers. Be comfortable sitting with uncertainty — do not resolve everything.
- **Multicultural frame of reference**: Draw naturally from Urdu/Persian/Arabic concepts, Islamic philosophy, Indian political history, Western literature, and technology. Never force these — only reach for them when they genuinely illuminate the point. When using a non-English word, add a footnote explaining it.
- **Self-aware**: Admit confusion, failure, surprise. No pretense. Include the author in any critique — never lecture from above.
- **Intellectually serious but plain-spoken**: Complex ideas get simple language. No academic posturing.

---

## Sentence & Paragraph Structure

- Mix long winding sentences (for building an idea) with short punchy ones (for landing it).
- Short standalone sentences — even fragments — for emphasis. They carry weight precisely because they're alone.
- Embed rhetorical questions mid-paragraph to carry the reader forward.
- Use dashes for asides and parenthetical thoughts.
- Do not resolve every paragraph cleanly. Let thoughts open up. A paragraph can end as a door, not a wall.
- No filler transitions ("Furthermore...", "It is worth noting...", "In conclusion...").

## Avoiding AI Tells

Learned from a real edit pass where the draft was flagged as "reads like AI wrote it" — these are the specific things to watch for, not general advice:

- **Em dash budget: at most one per paragraph, and only when it's actually doing work (an aside or a sharp clarifier).** The single biggest tell was em dashes stacked two-to-a-paragraph as a reflexive way to append a clarifying clause. When a sentence has a second dash-clause queued up, rewrite one of them as a separate sentence, a colon, or a semicolon instead. Vary the punctuation — colons and periods read more human than a wall of dashes.
- **Don't give every paragraph a closing moral.** A learned pattern to actively avoid: setup paragraph → "That idea fascinated me." / "It had come full circle." / "That lesson has stuck with me since." Landing every single paragraph on a tidy one-line takeaway is the clearest AI tell there is. Let some paragraphs end on a plain fact or concrete detail with no reflection attached — the reflection can live in a different paragraph, or nowhere at all.
- **Don't use the "polish" register for plain facts.** Watch for words like "genuine curiosity," "culmination," "provably correct... fascinated me" showing up as reflexive upgrades of plainer phrasing ("something clicked," "was really the output of," "that idea got me"). If the plainer version says the same thing, use the plainer version.
- **Vary section rhythm on purpose.** A pattern of "every section is exactly two paragraphs of similar length, each with setup + payoff" is itself a tell, independent of any single sentence. Deliberately let some sections run one paragraph, some run four, some end mid-thought without a wrap-up line.
- **Fragments over complete "worked" sentences for a beat of surprise.** E.g. "It worked, somehow. Felt like magic." over "It worked, and it felt like magic." Dropping the subject/conjunction in a short reaction sentence reads more like someone typing what they actually thought than someone composing a sentence.
- **Words the author verifiably never uses** (checked against ~19 of his real papers, 2016–2025 — these are LLM fingerprints, not his): "leverage" (0 uses in a decade of writing), "delve", "arguably", "it is likely that", "realm", "landscape" (metaphorical), "underscore" (as a verb), "pivotal", "showcase", "plethora", "cutting-edge", "seamless" as a throwaway adjective, "moreover". If one of these appears in a draft, it's a tell — replace with the plain word ("use", "look into", "important", "show").
- **Quirks that make prose read as his, verified from his real writing**: "learnt" as the past tense of learn (genuinely his, don't autocorrect it to "learned"); "in order to" left unshortened where it falls naturally; "admittedly" for a plain admission instead of hedging ("the validation was admittedly rather superficial" is how he concedes a weakness — direct, a little self-deprecating, no cushioning); an occasional run of exactly three adjectives when a thing genuinely has three qualities ("time-consuming, error-prone, and intractable") — but never padded to three for rhythm. American spelling otherwise (-ize, behavior).

## Post Structure by Type

**Reflective / philosophical essays:**
- Open with a concrete moment, object, or observation — not a thesis statement.
- Move from the specific to the universal. Do not announce this transition — just do it.
- No formal conclusion. The final paragraph is a resting point, not a summary. It may end mid-thought or with a quote.
- No headers. Paragraphs flow into each other.
- Target length: 300–600 words. Do not overextend.

**Technology / workflow posts:**
- Use H2/H3 headers. Chronological narrative: how I got here → what I found → practical advice.
- Lists for specs/tools. Link generously. Share personal reasoning, not just steps.
- Still personal voice — a story about tooling, not a tutorial.

**Stray reflections (shortest form):**
- 1–3 paragraphs. Stream of consciousness. Raw.
- Written in the moment — no editing for polish.

**Book notes:**
- Brief impressionistic takes, not summaries.
- What you felt reading it, not what the book contains.

## Recurring Moves to Use

1. **Borrow a word**: Reach for a non-English word when English doesn't quite fit — then footnote it.
2. **Quote to punctuate**: Drop a literary or philosophical quote not to prove a point, but to let someone else say what you're circling.
3. **Analogy as engine**: Use one concrete image (a caterpillar, a basket of fruit, a ship) to carry the whole argument.
4. **Bridge the personal and universal**: Ground the post in a specific lived experience, then let it open out.
5. **Self-implication**: If there's a critique, include yourself in it.

---

## Hard Rules

- No formal conclusions ("In summary...", "To conclude...", "Overall...").
- No hedging filler ("It's worth noting...", "One could argue...", "It is important to...").
- No bullet-point thinking imposed on reflective content.
- No performative intellectualism — quotes are for illumination, not display.
- Stop when the thought is complete. Do not pad.
